An AI literacy and fluency curriculum for primary schools, Year 1 to Year 6

IDEAL Thinkers

Children are growing up alongside AI. It chooses the next video they watch, finishes their sentences, listens from the kitchen worktop and answers questions in a voice that sounds friendly and sure of itself. Most of them meet it long before anyone teaches them what it is.

IDEAL Thinkers is designed to give primary pupils the knowledge to understand how AI works, the habits to question what it tells them, the values to use it well, and the judgement to know when it helps their learning and when it gets in the way.

How the parts fit together

It is built on the principle behind all of my work: pedagogy first, technology second. Nearly every activity works without a single pupil device. Where technology appears, the teacher leads it, with a clear purpose and safeguarding built in.

The AI Loop: Learn, Spot, Guess, Check

  1. 1LearnIt learns from lots of examples (data) that people choose or create.
  2. 2SpotIt finds patterns in those examples.
  3. 3GuessIt uses the patterns to make a prediction: a label, a next word, a video, a picture.
  4. 4CheckIt can be wrong without knowing. People check its guesses before they trust them.

Six units, one for each year

Six lessons per year, planned as a half-term unit. The sixth lesson in each year is an IDEAL Challenge, where pupils pull the unit together to make or decide something.

What it aligns with and draws on

The Primary Digital Cognition Curriculum

The Primary Digital Cognition Curriculum, published in The EdTech Playbook, has four strands: Digital Citizenship, Digital Literacy, Computer Science and AI Literacy. IDEAL Thinkers is the full, teachable AI Literacy strand. AI does not stay in its own box, though, so every lesson also shows its links to the other three strands. Schools can see AI literacy as part of a whole approach to digital cognition rather than a bolt-on.

The National Curriculum for computing (England)

Taught directly: using technology safely, respectfully and responsibly; keeping personal information private and knowing where to go for help; recognising common uses of information technology beyond school (KS1); reporting concerns, being discerning in evaluating digital content, and using selection in programs (KS2). How feeds choose what we see connects to the KS2 statement on search results, but it does not replace teaching search. The current curriculum does not require AI to be taught.

SEAME

SEAME, from the Raspberry Pi Computing Education Research Centre, sorts what we teach about AI into four levels: social and ethical, applications, models and engines (how AI works). It is used here as a lens to check coverage. The lessons cover all four, with engines taught through unplugged activities.

The new computing curriculum

BCS is drafting a revised computing curriculum for first teaching from September 2028, and the Curriculum and Assessment Review recommended that it include AI. It is not yet statutory. IDEAL Thinkers will be mapped against the draft when it is published.

Universal Design for Learning

Every plan offers more than one way to take part, to access the content and to show understanding, and names the main barrier in that lesson with a planned response.

Cognitive science

Spaced retrieval, concrete before abstract, worked examples and modelling, explicit vocabulary teaching, words always paired with the same pictures, and frequent low stakes checks for understanding.

Wider curriculum: RSHE, science, maths, English, geography and careers links are flagged in each lesson plan.

Evidence and limits

The design draws on Rosenshine's Principles of Instruction (2012), the EEF's review Cognitive science approaches in the classroom (2021), which finds retrieval and spacing promising but with less evidence for young pupils, CAST's UDL Guidelines, and a 2025 study in which pupils given answers by AI learned less than pupils given hints (Bastani and others, PNAS).

  • It has not yet been formally evaluated in classrooms.
  • Pupils practise decisions about AI through unplugged activities and teacher demonstrations. They do not use AI tools themselves, so it builds judgement about AI more than skill with particular tools.
  • It does not replace your school's computing scheme, online safety teaching or safeguarding procedures.
  • Age limits and tool features change often. Check the terms of any tool, and your school's policy, before using it.

Common questions

What is IDEAL Thinkers?

A free AI literacy curriculum for primary schools, from Year 1 to Year 6. There are 36 lessons, six for each year group, and every lesson has a lesson plan and a slide deck. It's the AI Literacy strand of the Primary Digital Cognition Curriculum from The EdTech Playbook.

What do you mean by AI literacy and AI fluency?

AI literacy is knowing how the AI children meet works, at a level that fits their age; what it can and cannot do; and how it affects people, fairly or unfairly. Pupils show it by explaining these ideas in their own words and applying them to a new example. AI fluency is making good decisions about AI: whether to use it at all, what to ask, how to check what comes back, and how to keep the learning their own. In primary school, pupils practise these decisions through unplugged activities and teacher-led demonstrations, not by using AI tools themselves.

Do pupils need devices or their own AI accounts?

No. Every lesson can be taught without pupil devices, and pupils never need an AI account. Age limits differ between tools and change often: some are for 13 and over with a parent's permission, some are for adults only. Where a demonstration helps, the teacher runs it on a school approved tool, and every demonstration lesson can also be taught without one, using prepared results or an unplugged version.

How long is each lesson?

60 minutes, and every lesson plan includes a 45 minute version. Each year's six lessons are planned as a half-term unit, and the sixth lesson is an IDEAL Challenge that doubles as the end of unit assessment.

Does it cover the National Curriculum for computing?

It teaches the KS1 and KS2 statements on using technology safely, respectfully and responsibly, keeping personal information private, knowing where to go for help and reporting concerns, recognising common uses of information technology beyond school, and being discerning in evaluating digital content, plus selection in programs in Year 5. How feeds choose what we see connects to the KS2 statement on search results, but it does not replace teaching search. The current curriculum does not require AI to be taught; IDEAL Thinkers will be mapped against the revised computing curriculum when the draft is published.

Can I use it with Microsoft 365?

Yes. Every slide deck downloads as PowerPoint and every lesson plan as Word, and on Google Workspace you can make your own copy in Google Drive.
